Sprinkle sand on a plate and bow its edge: the sand gathers on the nodal
lines of the plate's standing wave. For a mode $W = \cos(k_x x)\cos(k_y y)$
those lines are a grid — the cell boundaries. midi-sink cannot gather ink —
every operator here preserves area exactly, and Liouville's theorem says an
area-preserving map never changes density — but it can **stretch**, and the
Chladni operator stretches the ink along the edges of the keys.

**The cells are the eddies.** The layout's keys are circles — the ones the
tablets draw — and every one of them is an eddy. A texel inside a key turns
about the key's centre by

$$
\theta \,\big(4\rho^2(1-\rho^2)\big)^2,\qquad \rho = r/R,
$$

zero at the centre, full at $\rho = 1/\sqrt 2$, zero again at the rim with
zero slope, so the note's drop rests where it fell and is wound from its
edge while the water between the keys never moves. A rotation preserves
$r$, so the map has $\det J = 1$ inside every key and is continuous across
the touching rims: **class exact**, and the negative pass is the exact
inverse. Layouts that draw no keys — the circle of fifths, the rolls, the
theremin — take the largest circle at each note that touches no neighbour's.

**Two senses.** Neighbouring keys counter-rotate on the layout's own
checkerboard; the balance control turns the odd keys' sense from opposite
through still to the same.

**The inverse Chladni.** A second mode sums every key's ring into one
divergence-free flow instead of turning each key alone. The keys may then
grow past their circles and overlap, and the water between them is stirred
by both neighbours — the burst's class, area-preserving to first order, a
pass at a time.

## Invariants

* **Exact.** Measured on the desktop harness after 150 stirred frames: the
  key centres and the corners between keys move less than a quarter texel
  while the rings move sixteen; a ring round a centre turns 1.45 rad and
  stretches 0.09, a ring round a corner does nothing.
* **The eddies are the keys.** The stir's discs are the layout probe's
  cells, same centres and radius — the geometry the tablets draw and the
  fingers touch.
* **It bakes, and only bakes.** The flow writes into the field while the
  stir is held, a pass whenever it carries two quanta of displacement,
  never an absolute. The dip is the undo.
* **The ingress rule** applies where a key is clipped by the canvas edge.

## Ownership

* **The stir** (CC 106 on the desktop, *Stir (A)* in the settings) and
  **the balance** (CC 107, *Balance (B)*), unmapped in the core; *Cell
  size*, the eddy's disc as a fraction of the key (capped at the key in the
  exact mode); the mode, *Discs (exact)* or *Inverse Chladni*. Under the
  Anod medium's binding table the **per-note bend plays the stir**: its
  distance from centre sets the rate, its sign the sense — a bend down
  after a bend up is the inverse pass, so a vibrato stirs back and forth
  and comes home; the last bend written wins across voices, and a CC mapped
  to the stir overrides.
* **`sumi_add_chladni(psi, balance, s_x, x_0, s_y, y_0)`** as a gesture: one
  step of the two-wave cellular flow on a lattice of its own; a negative
  `psi` applies the exact inverse.

## The cellular flow, kept as a gesture

The first Chladni was the **Taylor–Green vortex**, the cellular flow whose
stream function is the plate's mode shape itself,

$$
\psi = \Psi\,\cos\big(k_x(x - x_0)\big)\cos\big(k_y(y - y_0)\big),
$$

an exact solution of the Navier–Stokes equations: eddies in the cells,
neighbours turning opposite ways, separatrices exactly on $\psi = 0$ — the
nodal lines. The product is not separable, but
$\cos u\cos v = \tfrac12[\cos(u - v) + \cos(u + v)]$ is a sum of two waves,
each a function of one *diagonal* coordinate, and the flow of such a wave is
a pure shear along the direction where that coordinate is constant. One
step of the flow is two exact shears — the kick-drift splitting of a
symplectic integrator — and its exact inverse is both shears negated in
reversed order (a sign flip alone is not: crossed shears do not commute).
That flow lives on as the gesture above; the layout's keys replaced it as
the stirred figure because the plate the player sees is made of circles,
not of a lattice's separatrices.

## Lineage

Ernst Chladni's figures (1787) are the picture; the exact rotation inside a
disc is Rankine's vortex with a smooth profile, the operator the vortex
family already used; Taylor and Green's cellular vortex (1937) is the
two-wave gesture, split into two exact shears; and Aref and Ottino's chaotic
advection is why iterating either winds the ink.
