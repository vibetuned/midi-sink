Sprinkle sand on a plate and bow its edge: the sand gathers on the nodal
lines of the plate's standing wave. For a mode $W = \cos(k_x x)\cos(k_y y)$
those lines are a grid — the cell boundaries. midi-sink cannot gather ink:
every operator here preserves area exactly, and Liouville's theorem says an
area-preserving map never changes density. What it can do is **stretch**. The
Chladni operator drives the water with the **Taylor–Green vortex**, the
cellular flow whose stream function is the mode shape itself,

$$
\psi = \Psi\,\cos\big(k_x(x - x_0)\big)\cos\big(k_y(y - y_0)\big),
$$

an exact solution of the Navier–Stokes equations. Its eddies sit in the
cells, neighbours turning opposite ways, and its separatrices lie exactly on
$\psi = 0$ — the nodal lines. Iterated, the flow spins the ink at each cell
centre in place and draws the ink near the boundaries out into filaments
along them: the figure forms where the sand would have settled, by
stretching instead of gathering.

**Two exact shears.** The product is not separable, but
$\cos u\cos v = \tfrac12[\cos(u - v) + \cos(u + v)]$ is a sum of two waves,
each a function of one *diagonal* coordinate, and the flow of such a wave is
a pure shear along the direction where that coordinate is constant. Each
shear is exact — the profile is constant along the shear — so one step of
the flow is two exact passes and $\det J = 1$ at any amplitude: the
kick-drift splitting of a symplectic integrator, **class exact**. The exact
inverse of a step is both shears negated in reversed order; a sign flip alone
is not, because crossed shears do not commute, and a headless test keeps the
"simultaneous" form from ever being written.

**The layout is the plate.** The lattice is the layout's cell lattice: a
note's drop sits at a cell centre and spins there like a Rankine core, and
the grid the player sees is the grid the figure outlines. On the Jankó and
the piano grid the lattice runs at half a column, so the staggered cells and
the accidentals are eddies too. Pressure only drives the stirring; the
geometry is the layout's. The circle of fifths and the rolls have no drawn
cells and take the largest imaginary cell that does not touch a neighbour's:
the fifths their octave-ring spacing, centred on the circle; the rolls one
semitone of their pitch axis.

**Cell size.** The eddies' pitch is a multiple of the layout's cell, anchored
on the cells: one eddy per cell by default, four per cell at a half, a cell
and a half at the top of the range.

## Invariants

* **Exact.** Measured on the desktop harness: one step moves the interior
  pre-image by several texels and the step with its inverse leaves a
  fraction of one, the resampler's floor. The inverse is `sumi_add_chladni`
  with a negative amplitude — the *order* of the shears reversed, not just
  their sign.
* **The fixed points are the layout's.** After a hundred and fifty stirred
  frames on the chromatic grid the cell centres and corners have not moved
  while the boundary midpoints have; a ring round a centre turns and a ring
  round a corner stretches.
* **It bakes, and only bakes.** The flow writes into the field while the stir
  is held, one step per frame at the rate the control sets, never an
  absolute. The dip is the undo.
* **The ingress rule** (fresh water where a shear leaves the canvas) applies
  as to the ripple, so the operator cannot duplicate boundary ink.

## Ownership

* **`SUMI_CTL_CHLADNI_A`** (the stirring rate) and **`_B`** (the balance:
  0 the cellular flow, ½ a single diagonal wave, 1 the cells reversed),
  unmapped in the core, CC 106 and 107 on the desktop; `chladni_cell`, the
  eddies' pitch as a multiple of the layout's cell. Under the Anod medium's
  binding table poly pressure will drive the stir (step 42).
* **`sumi_add_chladni(psi, balance, s_x, x_0, s_y, y_0)`** as a gesture: one
  step of the flow on a lattice of its own; a negative `psi` applies the
  exact inverse.

## Lineage

Ernst Chladni's figures (1787) are the picture;
Geoffrey Ingram Taylor and A. E. Green's cellular vortex (1937) is the flow
whose stream function is the plate's mode shape; the shear-then-shear
splitting — whose ordering is exactly what keeps it symplectic — is the
mathematics that draws the figure on water without a fold; and Aref and
Ottino's chaotic advection is why iterating it stretches the ink along the
separatrices. Both shears are the ripple's family, turned to the diagonals.
